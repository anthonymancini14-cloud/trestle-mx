# Maintenance planning practice

General planning and scheduling principles widely taught in the reliability profession (for example in SMRP's body of knowledge and planner-scheduler training). Paraphrased.

## What a good job plan contains

1. **Scope** a craftsperson can execute without a second trip: what is being done and what "done" means.
2. **Safety first:** permits, isolation points, job-specific hazards and controls.
3. **Ordered steps** with craft, crew size and hours per step. Estimate hours per person; crew-hours = hours x crew size.
4. **Materials** with storeroom part numbers and quantities, checked against stock before scheduling. Non-stock items flagged with lead time.
5. **Special tools and equipment:** lifting gear rated for the load, alignment tools, heaters, test instruments, scaffolding.
6. **Acceptance criteria** that are measurable: vibration, temperature, leakage, alignment tolerance, test results.
7. **Coordination:** operations handoff, equipment redundancy, contractor support.
8. **Feedback:** the closing debrief feeds the next plan. A repeat job should start from the last job's actual hours and findings, not from scratch.

## Planner habits

- Plan the job, not the person: assume a qualified craftsperson, do not write a training manual.
- Kit parts before the job is scheduled. A job is "ready" only when parts, permits and access are confirmed.
- Use history. If the last similar job overran, find out why and plan for it.
- Write open questions down instead of guessing. The field walkdown answers them.
- Separate what is known from what is assumed about failure cause.

## Common metrics a plan influences

- Schedule compliance (scheduled work completed as scheduled).
- Planned work as a share of total maintenance hours.
- Estimate accuracy (planned vs actual hours).
- Rework and repeat failures within a short window after the job.
