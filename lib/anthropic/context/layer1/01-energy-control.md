# Energy control (lockout/tagout)

Reference: OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147, The Control of Hazardous Energy. Paraphrased planning guidance, not the regulation text. The site's written energy control procedure for the specific equipment always governs.

## What a job plan must cover

- **Every energy source, not just the motor.** Electrical, mechanical (rotating, gravity, springs), hydraulic, pneumatic, process pressure, thermal, chemical. Pumps typically need electrical isolation plus suction and discharge valve isolation, a casing drain or vent, and sometimes seal flush or cooling water isolation.
- **Stored and residual energy.** Bleed pressure, drain liquids, let hot equipment cool, block or lower elevated parts, discharge capacitors in drives. A check valve is not an isolation point; a reverse-rotating pump is a real hazard.
- **Verification of isolation.** After locks are applied and before work starts: attempt a start from the local and remote controls, verify zero pressure on a gauge or at a bleed, and for electrical work test for absence of voltage with a verified meter.
- **Group lockout** when several crafts work under one isolation: a primary authorized person, a group lock box, and each worker's personal lock.
- **Shift change and contractor handoff**, if the job spans shifts.
- **Return to service sequence:** tools and people clear, guards on, locks removed by their owners only, controlled restart, and who signs the work order off.

## Planner red flags

- A plan that lists "LOTO" as a single step with no named isolation points.
- Isolation points taken from a drawing that has not been walked down.
- Valve isolation relying on a single valve for high-energy or hazardous fluid (consider double block and bleed or blinding).
- Drives and VFDs: stored energy in the DC bus; observe the drive's posted discharge time.
